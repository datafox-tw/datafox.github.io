# My Story with LinkedIn

Although I can no longer find the notes I took in my freshman year, I still remember during my 'Introduction to Finance' class, amidst all the colorful and fancy job titles, I noticed a common pattern: successful people were all actively using LinkedIn. Whether they were investment analysts, entrepreneurs, asset managers, or tech PMs, having a professional presence coupled with a high number of connections, while requiring time to cultivate, was essentially all upside with no downside.
Here are two insights I've gained over the past few years:

1. Even if you currently have 'nothing substantial,' rack your brain to list your past experiences
When I first entered university, some people around me were already on LinkedIn. I graduated from a private vocational high school, and back then, I had no connection to finance whatsoever and truly no outstanding achievements. I remember when I was forced to write the first draft of my resume in class, all I could squeeze onto it were: tutored, ranked first in my high school class, TOEIC and GEPT scores... information that now seems trivial.
But as time passed, I gradually gained experience from clubs, internships, workshops, and coursework. Recently, to make my profile more concise, I even deleted many 'flotsam and jetsam' that I once considered important, such as student council experience or being a member of a certain seminar. During the revision process, I felt the satisfaction of refining and removing unnecessary parts, which is also the best evidence of growth.
University students often think, 'Who would want to connect with me?' and retreat to Instagram or Threads. But if you can regularly update your thoughts, it's not just practicing to adapt to LinkedIn's professional environment, but also accumulating your personal perspectives. A profile with content is always better than an empty shell; at least it gives headhunters or future companies more angles to understand you when they want to. The first step is always the hardest. We can start by creating a 'lite version,' then observe how KOLs (Key Opinion Leaders) we admire write their profiles, and gradually fine-tune it into our own unique style.

Pro Tip: Pre-set a career goal you wish to achieve and incorporate keywords into your headline. Even if you haven't achieved it yet, this is a form of 'signaling,' letting others see that you are ambitious and interested in that field, and that effect is already sufficient.

2. Don't be shy about adding connections relentlessly
I heard from an older student in my freshman year: Don't worry about what your resume looks like, and certainly don't be afraid of rejection. As long as your connections exceed 500+ (considered the golden number on LinkedIn), you'll transition from 'you adding others' to 'others actively adding you'.
My approach back then was straightforward: target keywords (e.g., Finance), then keep sending connection requests; or look for older students from the same university or clubs. Don't worry about what happens if you get rejected. After a few months, when your connections grow, or if you land a great internship, those who initially ignored you might even have a change of heart (chuckle).
In short, if you're reading this article on LinkedIn, don't hesitate—go create your connections! (Or feel free to add me too; I always accept!)
