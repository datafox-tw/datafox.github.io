TITLE: Why "Talkative" AI Agents Can Be a Disaster in Financial Scenarios
DESCRIPTION: This article explores why, despite the widespread belief that multi-step reasoning and multi-agent architectures enhance AI performance, "talkative" AI agents can be a disaster in financial scenarios. It deeply analyzes how, for quantitative financial validation that demands extreme precision—such as the mere 46% Pass@1 accuracy in JPMorgan's research—more dialogue actually diminishes effectiveness. The piece reveals how low accuracy and the "reflection trap" limit the application of AI in finance.
BODY:

In the world of LLMs, we often hear that "Multi-step Reasoning" or "Multi-agent" architectures can significantly improve performance. For scenarios that require sparking creativity and brainstorming, allowing Insights Agents to "say a few more words" indeed expands semantic boundaries and ignites unexpected sparks.

**However, in "quantitative financial validation" scenarios that demand extreme precision, being talkative can actually lead to terrible performance.**

I recently read JPMorgan's paper *《A Multi-Agent Framework for Quantitative Finance》*, published at EMNLP 2025. This paper proposes a framework that includes "Insights Agents" such as Data Summarizer, Finance Expert, and Query Refiner, attempting to enhance the performance of the Base Agent by incorporating more financial knowledge and data preprocessing.

While this research academically explores the possibilities of complex architectures, from a practical standpoint, I must make a strong statement: **this is absolutely not a production system that JPMorgan would actually run internally.**

The reason is simple: **the accuracy (Pass@1) is too low.**

### **1. 46% Accuracy: This is Just a Baseline**
According to the paper's data, even with the layering of so many Insights Agents, the overall Pass@1 accuracy is only **46%** (compared to a single agent's 39.59%). When dealing with "Hard" level financial problems, the accuracy is even worse. For quantitative finance, a field where a single sign error can lead to a huge disparity, such performance is far from acceptable.

### **2. The "Reflection Trap"**
The paper mentions an interesting phenomenon: in certain simple (Easy) tasks, the Pass@5 or Pass@10 metrics after incorporating a reflection mechanism are actually lower than without it. This further confirms that if an Agent lacks external physical validation (e.g., rigid symbolic checks or unit tests), it is merely "randomly self-correcting with confidence," often resulting in a worse outcome.

### **3. The Danger of Lacking Ground Truth**
In real business scenarios, if we don't have the ground truth, this "multi-agent, verbose" architecture can be highly misleading. It will spew out a plethora of seemingly professional financial jargon, generated code, and detailed reflection logs, creating the illusion for users that "it understands a lot." However, when you delve into its computational logic, you might find that it has even misinterpreted field definitions.

**Core Reflection:**
Financial validation requires **Symbolic Precision** and **rigorous logical verification**, not more verbose semantic padding. We cannot rely on LLMs' "linguistic talent" to solve problems of "logical computation."

This type of paper serves as a suitable baseline to understand the limitations of Agent architectures, but on the path to AGI, we need more hardcore validation frameworks, rather than merely making Agents more "talkative."
