TITLE: The "Elementary Math" Check That Even Top Conference Papers Can't Escape 🔍
DESCRIPTION: An ACL top conference paper surprisingly contained an arithmetic error that even the reviewers missed. Despite claiming to evaluate "financial reasoning" capabilities, it couldn't even get basic addition and subtraction right. The author shares the discovery process, emphasizing that even top-tier research can err. This inspires us to think critically, not blindly trust authority, and highlights the importance of rigorous verification frameworks for AI and high-risk domains.
BODY:

Recently, while deep-diving into a Main Conference paper published at ACL (a top-tier conference in Computational Linguistics), something utterly amusing yet frustrating occurred.

To develop my new paper framework, I thoroughly scrutinized the paper's open-source GitHub code and raw data distribution. The result? I uncovered a fundamental flaw that even the three original reviewers missed: this paper, which claimed to evaluate "financial reasoning capabilities," couldn't even get basic addition and subtraction right!

The core issue was:
"The total number of questions N claimed by the paper did not equal the sum of its listed components A + B."
Simply put, the authors meticulously broke down the data sources in both the main text and the appendix, but when these two numbers were added together, they astonishingly didn't match the total count they repeatedly emphasized in the abstract, main text, and figures.

When I wrote to the first author for verification, they sincerely apologized, admitted it was a typo, and thanked me for helping clarify the data consistency.

This incident offered me two major insights:
1️⃣ Don't Blindly Trust Authority (Critical Thinking): Even papers published in top-tier conferences and peer-reviewed can still contain extremely basic errors. If I had chosen to "rationalize" this number, my experimental baseline would have been skewed from the very beginning.

2️⃣ Why We Need Verification Frameworks: The subject of this paper was "financial numerical reasoning," yet the author themselves stumbled on numerical reasoning. This is precisely my research interest—we need a more rigorous, Human-in-the-Loop mechanism to prevent AI (or even human authors) from "randomly guessing intentions" or generating logical hallucinations in such high-stakes domains.

I'm grateful for the author's honest response; this discrepancy of 90 questions unexpectedly became my ticket to establishing an academic connection with a top researcher.

Moving forward, I'll carry this "spirit of skepticism" and continue to sharpen my verification framework. 🛠️
